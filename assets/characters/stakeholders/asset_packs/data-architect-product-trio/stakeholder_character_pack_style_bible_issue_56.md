# Stakeholder Character Pack Style Bible

**Project:** *Adventures of Patch*  
**Adventure issue:** #56  
**Asset pack:** Stakeholder character pack  
**Reference basis:** Derived from the attached stakeholder character images in this conversation. Not live repo-verified in this pass.

## 1. Pack Identity

The stakeholder character pack defines the civilian stakeholder side of the tournament: thoughtful, competent people who bring process knowledge, product framing, data interpretation, and systems reasoning into the adventure. These characters are not judges, referees, athletes, or route officials. They are the people who help explain why a decision matters, how evidence is organized, what tradeoffs exist, and which route or default is reasonable.

The visual identity should read as:

- friendly professional stakeholders;
- contemporary but softened into the Adventures editorial style;
- smart-casual rather than uniformed;
- approachable, helpful, and engaged;
- associated with tablets, clipboards, charts, maps, diagrams, calendars, notes, and badges;
- clear enough to be reused as individual character assets or grouped in scenes.

The stakeholder cast should feel like a good-natured product/data/architecture working group visiting a cheerful outdoor tournament, not like corporate executives, stern authorities, or generic office workers.

## 2. Relationship to the Wider Issue #56 Visual System

This pack belongs to the same Issue #56 tournament world as the environment, judges/officials, dugout, and canonical costume packs. The stakeholder characters should share the same clean vector-adjacent rendering, warm light, readable forms, soft painterly shading, and optimistic editorial tone.

They differ from the judges and officials in costume language. Judges/officials use cream polo shirts, khaki trousers, whistles, blue-and-gold event insignia, medals, flags, and formal tournament props. Stakeholders use civilian professional clothing, ID badges, tablets, planning documents, analytics props, process cards, lanyards, and softer accent colours.

They differ from Patch, Bit, and Bot in narrative function. Patch is the autonomous protagonist; Bit and Bot are companion/rival robot figures. Stakeholders are human process participants who provide context, constraints, evidence, and interpretation.

## 3. Core Character Archetypes

The pack currently establishes three stakeholder archetypes.

### Data Analyst

The data analyst is warm, organized, and quietly confident. She is visually associated with purple and gold analytics materials: a calendar-style planning board, filter icon, charts, reports, lanyard badge, pen, and structured sheets.

Preserve these cues:

- young adult woman with warm brown skin tone;
- large round glasses;
- brown curly hair in a loose high bun with stray curls;
- gold hoop earrings and small necklace;
- cream oversized cardigan over a white top;
- high-waisted brown trousers, rolled cuffs, mustard socks, light cream sneakers;
- purple lanyard and badge with lightbulb-style icon;
- clipboard or tablet showing calendar grid, filter funnel, check marks, or chart blocks.

Her personality should read as observant, patient, detail-oriented, and helpful. She explains patterns without being cold or robotic.

### Product Manager

The product manager is organized, communicative, and decision-oriented. He is associated with roadmaps, timeline boards, feature cards, check marks, coloured status blocks, and a practical lanyard badge.

Preserve these cues:

- young adult man with curly dark brown hair;
- black rectangular glasses;
- light blue button-up shirt with rolled sleeves;
- dark navy tie;
- dark navy trousers;
- brown belt and brown dress shoes;
- lanyard and badge with lightbulb/idea mark;
- tablet or clipboard showing a roadmap, timeline, coloured status cards, check boxes, and simple planning icons.

His personality should read as friendly, explanatory, and alignment-focused. He is the one pointing to priorities, showing what is complete, and helping turn ambiguity into a plan.

### Software Architect

The software architect is calm, technical, and systems-minded. He is associated with diagrams, blueprints, tablets, rolled plans, network/system charts, notebooks, dark lanyards, and deeper blue tones.

Preserve these cues:

- adult man with dark curly hair and a grey side streak;
- neatly trimmed beard;
- black rectangular glasses;
- olive-brown blazer over a cream or white shirt;
- dark navy trousers;
- brown casual shoes;
- blue lanyard and badge with a hexagonal system icon;
- tablet showing node diagrams, cloud icon, data flow boxes, cube/module symbol, database cylinder, arrows, or architecture blocks;
- optional rolled blueprint held under one arm.

His personality should read as approachable and technically grounded, not aloof. He can gesture while explaining a system, dependency, or structure.

## 4. Shared Stakeholder Design Language

All stakeholder characters should share these design principles:

- full-body isolated figures on clean white or very light backgrounds for source assets;
- relaxed upright poses, gentle smiles, and open hand gestures;
- slightly oversized expressive eyes and simplified facial features;
- neat but non-uniform clothing;
- visible lanyard badges as their shared stakeholder credential;
- props held close to the body and readable at thumbnail scale;
- professional materials simplified into icons, blocks, check marks, charts, and diagrams rather than tiny real text;
- warm, non-threatening body language.

The stakeholders should feel like a coherent cast while retaining distinct roles through colour, clothing, props, and iconography.

## 5. Costume and Wardrobe Rules

Stakeholder clothing should be smart-casual, soft, and practical for a field-side tournament. Avoid formal suits, hard corporate styling, sports uniforms, or judge uniforms.

Useful wardrobe ingredients:

- cardigans, rolled-sleeve shirts, blazers, clean T-shirts, ties used casually, neat trousers, practical sneakers or casual shoes;
- lanyards and ID badges with simple role icons;
- muted neutrals paired with one role accent colour;
- soft fabric folds, cuffs, collars, and relaxed layering.

Role colour accents should remain consistent:

- Data Analyst: purple, lavender, cream, warm brown, gold/yellow chart accents.
- Product Manager: light blue, navy, green/orange/purple roadmap cards, brown leather details.
- Software Architect: olive-brown, navy, cream, blue diagram accents, black tablet frame.

Do not give stakeholders the judges’ cream polo/khaki/whistle identity unless the scene explicitly involves an official role. Do not give them Patch’s teal costume, Bit/Bot robot colours, or athlete race bibs.

## 6. Prop Language

Stakeholder props carry most of the pack’s narrative specificity. They should be simplified but unmistakable.

### Data Analyst Props

- tablet or clipboard with calendar grid;
- filter funnel icon;
- check marks;
- donut charts, bar charts, small report pages;
- purple lanyard badge with lightbulb icon;
- cream-and-purple pen;
- clean cards showing analytics summaries.

These props should suggest filtering, interpreting, and checking evidence.

### Product Manager Props

- tablet or clipboard showing roadmap lanes;
- coloured feature cards or priority blocks;
- check boxes;
- timeline line with milestones;
- stakeholder cards, ID badge, notebook, sticky notes;
- icons for people, trophy/outcome, flag, question, chart, target, or web/page.

These props should suggest sequencing, alignment, prioritization, and acceptance criteria.

### Software Architect Props

- tablet showing system diagram;
- central cube/module with connected boxes;
- cloud and database icons;
- arrows showing dependencies or data flow;
- rolled blueprint;
- dark stylus or technical pen;
- badge with hexagon/module mark.

These props should suggest structure, system constraints, and how parts connect.

## 7. Facial Expression and Acting

Stakeholders should mostly express patient helpfulness. They are allowed to be thoughtful, curious, amused, gently explanatory, or politely concerned, but they should not look angry, villainous, defeated, smug, or bureaucratically obstructive.

Good acting choices include:

- pointing to a tablet while smiling;
- holding a clipboard toward the viewer;
- presenting a diagram with an open palm;
- holding supporting documents under one arm;
- looking at the viewer as if explaining something clearly;
- standing in a neutral three-quarter pose for reusable source clarity.

Avoid melodrama. These characters support the “reasonable defaults” tone: professional, good-humoured, and constructive.

## 8. Rendering Style

Use the clean Adventures editorial / vector-adjacent painterly style established across Issue #56:

- crisp dark linework with rounded forms;
- soft painterly shading on clothing, faces, and props;
- smooth gradients on tablets and paper;
- clear silhouette and generous white space for isolated character assets;
- simplified readable icons instead of dense interface detail;
- warm skin shading and gentle facial highlights;
- tidy edges suitable for compositing into decks or asset sheets.

Characters should look more polished and source-asset-ready than background crowd figures. Keep full-body proportions consistent across the pack.

## 9. Composition Patterns

Preferred asset compositions:

1. Hero character pose: full-body, three-quarter view, role prop held up, friendly expression.
2. Alternate character pose: same costume and identity, slightly different hand gesture or prop arrangement.
3. Prop sheet: role-specific tools arranged on a clean light background, with tablet/clipboard as the largest item and smaller documents, badges, notebooks, pens, or sticky notes around it.
4. Group scene integration: stakeholder characters can appear inside the dugout or near route paperwork, but should not overpower Patch or the location asset.

For isolated figures, keep the background white or softly off-white. For integrated scenes, use the stakeholder dugout and long-course world as the environmental base.

## 10. Colour Palette

This pack uses a softer stakeholder palette than the blue-and-gold official tournament assets.

Shared neutrals:

- warm cream and off-white for papers, shirts, cardigans, and tablets;
- dark navy for trousers, lanyards, tablet frames, and diagrams;
- warm brown for shoes, belts, hair, and some clothing;
- soft black for glasses, tablet frames, and outlines.

Role accents:

- purple and gold for data analysis;
- light blue, navy, green, orange, and lavender for product planning;
- olive-brown, slate blue, navy, and pale green for architecture diagrams.

Avoid overusing tournament royal blue and trophy gold in this pack except where the character is placed directly in event infrastructure. The stakeholders should relate to the tournament but not visually merge into the officials.

## 11. Badge and Icon Continuity

Lanyards and badges are important continuity markers. Each stakeholder should wear one, but the badge design can differ by role.

Preferred badge logic:

- Data Analyst: purple badge with lightbulb or insight icon.
- Product Manager: lightbulb/idea badge, often on a dark lanyard.
- Software Architect: blue hexagon/module badge.

The badge should be visible but not oversized. It identifies them as stakeholder participants, not race competitors.

## 12. Interface and Document Design

Screens and documents should be readable as concepts, not functioning UI. Use large simplified shapes and icon clusters.

Good screen/document motifs:

- calendar grid with highlighted row;
- filter funnel;
- check marks;
- roadmap lanes and milestone dots;
- coloured cards with short line marks;
- flowchart nodes and arrows;
- cloud/database/module icons;
- donut and bar charts;
- sticky notes with simple symbols.

Avoid tiny pseudo-text, real app branding, copyrighted interface patterns, excessive labels, or illegible clutter. The visual job is to convey the role quickly.

## 13. Relationship to Patch, Bit, and Bot

When shown with Patch, Bit, or Bot, stakeholders should remain human context providers. They can advise, ask clarifying questions, present evidence, or help interpret the tournament’s constraints. They should not become the protagonists.

Patch should remain visually distinct in teal canonical costume. Bit and Bot should remain robot companions/rivals with red and blue accents respectively. Stakeholders should not inherit robot markings, race numbers, antenna hoods, or mechanical elements.

The likely narrative relationship is collaborative: stakeholders supply observations and process structure while Patch reasons through the route or challenge.

## 14. What to Preserve

Preserve these elements across future stakeholder character images:

- clear role separation: data analyst, product manager, software architect;
- smart-casual civilian stakeholder clothing;
- lanyard badges as shared credential language;
- role-specific tablets/clipboards/props;
- friendly professional expressions;
- clean white-background source-asset presentation;
- simplified charts, diagrams, roadmaps, and checklists;
- warm, approachable Adventures rendering.

## 15. What to Avoid

Avoid:

- turning stakeholders into judges, referees, athletes, or medal officials;
- giving them identical uniforms;
- making them corporate, cold, stern, or villainous;
- overloading tablets with unreadable UI detail;
- using real brand logos or realistic software interfaces;
- removing lanyard/badge cues;
- changing core hair, glasses, clothing, or role-colour continuity between hero and alternate poses;
- making prop sheets too generic to indicate the stakeholder role.

## 17. Asset-Pack Summary

The stakeholder character pack gives Issue #56 its human process layer. These characters personify the different kinds of reasoning the adventure needs: data interpretation, product alignment, and system architecture. Their look should be warm, competent, and readable, with role-specific props doing much of the storytelling. They are not tournament authorities; they are constructive stakeholders who help Patch understand evidence, constraints, routes, and reasonable defaults.
