## 1. Purpose of the Pack

This asset pack defines the environmental language for *Patch and the Tournament of Reasonable Defaults*. It covers the outdoor tournament grounds, course trials, maze environments, height-standard apparatus, route barriers, start and finish infrastructure, signage, judging stations, and scenic countryside continuity.

The environment should feel like a cheerful, meticulously arranged countryside tournament rather than a modern stadium or a grim fantasy arena. It is organized, readable, and full of playful institutional detail. Every trial location should look official enough to support rules and scoring, but handmade enough to feel warm, local, and accessible.

The pack’s main job is to make the adventure world feel coherent across slides, source sheets, prompt boards, and later scene generation. Each environment should be clear enough to establish location, route logic, trial type, and mood at a glance.

## 2. Core Visual Identity

The visual style is clean Adventures editorial illustration with a vector-adjacent structure and light painterly finish. Forms are simplified and readable, with crisp linework, rounded silhouettes, soft shading, and a strong sense of sunny outdoor space.

The setting is an old-fashioned countryside athletic tournament: dirt paths, rolling green hills, pavilions, striped tents, bunting, banners, brass posts, rope barriers, flower beds, hedge mazes, start lines, finish gates, judging tables, and practical course markers. It should feel like a storybook sports day scaled up into a logic-and-rules adventure.

The emotional tone is bright, fair, and orderly. The tournament has constraints, routes, penalties, ambiguous paths, and judging infrastructure, but it should never feel hostile. The environment presents problems through layout and signage rather than danger or menace.

## 3. Palette

Primary colours:

- Deep tournament blue: banners, railings, barriers, tablecloths, course fabric, start pads, tent accents, and official branding.
- Warm gold and brass: finials, posts, laurel marks, trophy emblems, platform trim, ceremonial details, and sign hardware.
- Cream and parchment: signs, boards, paper maps, tent panels, checklist surfaces, and readable information areas.
- Warm dirt and tan: course paths, ground, wooden signs, podium stages, and neutral surfaces.
- Fresh countryside greens: hedges, grass, distant hills, bushes, trees, and flower-bed structure.
- Red, white, yellow, and green accents: bunting, flags, finish gates, lane markers, decision signage, tents, and hazard tape.
- Black and white: controlled use for road markings, readable sign text, checkered finish motifs, and striped apparatus.

Avoid neon colour, cold corporate grey, harsh industrial black, muddy low-light palettes, modern stadium branding, and aggressive hazard styling unless a specific trial element needs a small warning accent.

## 4. Lighting and Atmosphere

Lighting should be bright warm daylight, usually under an open blue sky with soft cumulus clouds. Shadows are visible enough to ground objects, but never dark or dramatic. The space should feel clear, healthy, and breathable.

Common atmospheric elements:

- Blue sky with rounded white clouds.
- Rolling hills fading into atmospheric distance.
- Evergreen trees and leafy hedges.
- Wildflowers, shrubs, and flower beds around course edges.
- Light breeze shown through flags, banners, and bunting.
- Soft distant tents or pavilions to imply a larger tournament site.

Avoid night scenes, storm light, fog, smoke, heavy bloom, harsh rim lighting, or theatrical spotlighting. The tournament should feel like it is being run in public, in fair conditions, with everything visible.

## 5. Linework, Shape Language, and Rendering

Linework is crisp, rounded, and confident. The environments should be detailed, but not fussy. Most objects should have clean silhouettes and readable functions even when scaled down.

Shape language:

- Paths curve gently and visibly through the image.
- Hedges are thick, rounded, and neatly maintained.
- Tents and pavilions use soft peaks, scalloped edges, and striped fabric.
- Posts, finials, and barriers are tidy and ceremonial.
- Signs are broad, flat, and readable, with high-contrast text or symbols.
- Apparatus is sturdy and slightly toy-like rather than aggressively realistic.

Rendering uses light painterly shading over clean forms. Grass, hedges, dirt, fabric, and wood can carry texture, but texture should support readability rather than overwhelm it. The style should not become photorealistic, hyper-rendered, or flat iconography.

## 6. Environment Design Principles

The environments should make tournament logic visible. A viewer should be able to understand that this is a rule-governed event without needing character narration.

Use environment to communicate:

- Routes and choices.
- Deadlines or countdown pressure.
- Standards and measurements.
- Validation and judging points.
- Ambiguity, false starts, or multiple plausible paths.
- Friendly institutional order.

The world should be visually generous. Even when an environment is a maze or a confusing route, it should be pleasant to look at: flowers, hedges, flags, signboards, and scenic hills soften the logic problem.

## 7. Tournament Branding

The recurring brand mark is a gold trophy with laurel branches, usually on deep blue fabric or signage. It appears across the environment as the shared institutional identity.

Brand surfaces include:

- Large vertical banners.
- Finish gates and overhead signs.
- Tablecloths and judging stations.
- Blue barriers and rail panels.
- Podium stages.
- Hedge maze signage.
- Start-line mats and flags.
- Official pavilions and tents.

Branding should feel consistent but not mechanically identical. It can be simplified on distant objects. The overall read matters more than exact pixel-level emblem repetition.

Text should be functional and sparse. Strong examples include “START”, “FINISH - 7 DAYS!”, “TODAY”, “INDUSTRY STANDARD”, and “HEDGE MAZE”. Avoid long explanatory signage, dense pseudo-text, or real-world corporate sponsor language.

## 8. Course One: Reasonable Defaults / Multi-Route Finish Course

This environment uses several winding paths, repeated finish gates, countdown banners, and penalty markers. It communicates choice, deadline pressure, and the absurdity of multiple routes all claiming to reach a finish.

Core elements:

- A broad start line in the foreground.
- Multiple parallel or branching dirt paths.
- Red, blue, and green finish gates reading “FINISH - 7 DAYS!” or similar.
- White penalty boards with negative numbers.
- “TODAY” marker sign near the start or route split.
- Brass posts, bunting, flower beds, route ropes, and tournament tents.
- Side banners that establish values such as fairness, clarity, acceptance, or stakeholder tournament identity.

Composition should make path structure readable. The hero version can use a wide, slightly elevated view so the viewer sees the whole route logic. Alternate views may focus on a smaller cluster of paths, finish gates, or confusing signposts.

Avoid making the route feel like a racetrack, motorway, or obstacle-course mud run. It is a ceremonial logic course, not a speed-race facility.

## 9. Course Two: Industry Standard Height Apparatus

This environment centers on a high-jump-like apparatus used as a standard-setting trial. It should look official, measurable, and slightly comic in its overdetermined seriousness.

Core elements:

- Blue padded landing mat.
- Upright measurement posts with scale markings and adjustment holes.
- Red-and-white upper bar.
- Yellow-and-black lower bar or caution-standard bar.
- Blue metal feet and brackets.
- Nearby wooden sign reading “INDUSTRY STANDARD”.
- Optional wooden ladder, tents, bunting, and distant tournament grounds.

The apparatus should look stable and functional, with clear construction. The measurements and brackets should imply official calibration, but the image should not become a technical diagram. Keep it toy-like, readable, and bright.

Avoid making the apparatus look dangerous, rusty, modern-industrial, gymnasium-like, or sterile. It belongs outside in a cheerful tournament field.

## 10. Course Three: Hedge Maze / Ambiguous Path Trial

The hedge maze environment expresses ambiguity, many plausible routes, and the problem of deciding what counts as “found” or “complete.” It should be lush, tidy, ceremonial, and legible.

Core elements:

- Tall bright-green hedge walls with rounded tops.
- Circular or structured maze geometry.
- Central or internal prize stations on blue-and-gold plinths.
- Trophy, sealed box, open chest, document stand, or similar trial objectives.
- Entrance arch or sign reading “HEDGE MAZE”.
- Red and blue tournament tents around the perimeter.
- Brass poles, bunting, banners, flowers, and viewing pavilions.
- For annotated variants: notebook page, hand-drawn maze map, dashed route arrows, question marks, checkmarks, and circled target icons.

Hero maze views should establish scale and setting. Overhead views should make the maze geometry clear while keeping the editorial storybook style. Annotated-map views should feel like a practical reasoning note, not a perfect digital map.

Avoid horror-maze atmosphere, overgrown neglect, dark forest tunnels, dungeon labyrinths, or a sterile infographic look.

## 11. Course Four: Long Route / False Line / Podium Context

This environment covers the broader tournament route and the problem of false or misleading completion markers. It has a more open countryside feel than the maze or course cluster.

Core elements:

- Long dirt road or course path stretching into rolling hills.
- Blue tournament barriers and white flags along the route.
- A bold start-line pad or marked road crossing.
- Potential false-line risk: a road crossing, painted white lines, barricaded hole, cones, hazard tape, or other misleading interruption.
- Open rural terrain, lake or rocks, distant hills, and tree clusters.
- Separate podium/judging area with blue-and-gold table, podium blocks, banners, tents, and floral borders.

This course should feel expansive and deceptively simple. The long path invites the viewer’s eye into the distance, while false-line elements create tension about what counts as the real finish or valid route.

Avoid urban roads, car traffic, harsh construction-site realism, dangerous disaster imagery, or clutter that makes the intended route illegible.

## 12. Signage and Text Rules

Environmental text must be large, sparse, and directly useful. It should read as part of the tournament, not as a caption pasted onto the image.

Good signage types:

- START / FINISH markers.
- Countdown banners.
- TODAY marker.
- Negative-number penalty boards.
- Industry Standard sign.
- Hedge Maze entrance sign.
- Value banners with short principle words.
- Simple arrows and direction signs.

Text should be set in strong block lettering on broad sign surfaces. It may be imperfectly hand-painted, but it must remain legible. Avoid tiny labels, paragraphs, legalistic wording, excessive pseudo-writing, or unclear symbolic clutter.

## 13. Materials and Props

Common materials:

- Packed dirt and gravel for paths.
- Painted wood for signs, gates, podiums, and plinths.
- Brass or gold-toned metal for posts, finials, and ceremonial railings.
- Blue cloth and canvas for banners, tablecloths, barriers, and tents.
- Cream canvas for tents, signs, and paper-like surfaces.
- Hedge greenery with rounded leaf texture.
- Painted metal for height apparatus, brackets, bars, and supports.
- Rope for barriers and bunting lines.

Objects should feel practical and handmade. They may be official, but they should never feel mass-produced corporate event furniture.

## 14. Composition Guidance

### Wide hero environments

Use a broad, slightly elevated composition when the route logic matters. The viewer should understand the whole site: foreground start or entrance, middle-ground trial structure, and background tournament infrastructure.

Good hero framing:

- Foreground anchor such as START line, gate, sign, or entrance.
- Clear course path leading the eye.
- Main trial structure centered or deliberately staged.
- Tents and banners to the sides for scale and continuity.
- Distant hills and sky for openness.

### Detail views

Use closer views for apparatus, lower bars, ladders, signs, road hazards, or judging-station details. Keep the main object large, readable, and unobstructed.

Good detail framing:

- Apparatus or sign fills the central frame.
- Background remains consistent but softened.
- Shadows ground the object.
- Relevant secondary props clarify use without cluttering the scene.

### Overhead or map views

Use overhead views for maze structure and annotated reasoning. Maintain the same warm style even when the composition becomes diagrammatic.

Good overhead/map framing:

- Maze or course geometry is clear.
- Targets or prize stations are visible.
- Annotations are secondary to the map, not visually chaotic.
- Notebook or paper surfaces have warm tactile texture.

## 15. Continuity Rules

Maintain these continuity points across related outputs:

- The tournament is outdoors in warm daylight.
- The main official identity is deep blue and gold with trophy/laurel branding.
- Paths are tan dirt or gravel with white painted edges or stone edging where useful.
- Tents are striped, usually red/cream or blue/cream.
- Bunting uses red, blue, yellow, white, and occasional green.
- Posts, rails, and finials lean brass/gold rather than steel grey.
- Flower beds and greenery soften every trial site.
- Course signage is readable, handmade, and tournament-branded.
- Environments are mostly character-free unless a scene-specific request asks for characters.
- Patch, Bit, Bot, judges, stakeholders, spectators, and officials should not be added to environment-only source assets unless explicitly requested.

Do not drift into modern Olympics, school athletics, corporate away-day, circus fairground chaos, medieval jousting lists, dystopian games, or generic fantasy garden maze imagery.

## 16. Do / Don’t

Do:

- Keep the environment bright, warm, and legible.
- Use visible route logic: starts, finishes, markers, path splits, standards, and signs.
- Preserve the blue-and-gold tournament identity.
- Use flowers, hedges, tents, and hills to keep the world cheerful.
- Make environmental assets broad enough for reuse in decks and prompt boards.
- Use sparse, functional text that reads clearly.
- Keep trial infrastructure official but handmade.

Don’t:

- Add characters to source environment assets unless asked.
- Turn the tournament into a modern stadium, racetrack, office event, or construction site.
- Use grim, dangerous, hostile, or militarized visual language.
- Overload the frame with unreadable signs or tiny labels.
- Make the course logic visually ambiguous when the image is meant to establish the course.
- Replace the tournament identity with unrelated team colours or sponsor branding.
- Use photorealism, flat sterile vector art, heavy anime exaggeration, or rough painterly chaos.

## 17. Prompt-Building Pattern

Use this structure for future prompts in this environment family:

> Create a clean editorial source image for the environment asset pack in *Patch and the Tournament of Reasonable Defaults*. Use the same simplified Adventures editorial / clean vector-adjacent style as the accepted tournament environment assets: cheerful old-fashioned countryside athletic tournament, warm daylight, blue-and-gold event branding, rounded confident linework, simplified readable forms, light painterly shading, and practical reusable source-asset clarity. Show [specific environment or trial location] as [wide hero / alternate course view / apparatus detail / overhead map / annotated notebook view]. Include [course-specific infrastructure: starts, finishes, signs, markers, hedges, apparatus, barriers, tents, banners, podium, or route hazards]. Keep the scene sunny, fair, well-organized, and readable. Use deep blue and gold tournament branding, cream signage, tan dirt paths, brass posts, bunting, striped tents, rolling green hills, and flowers. Do not include Patch, Bit, Bot, contestants, judges, officials, spectators, stakeholders, or extra characters unless explicitly requested.

## 18. Negative Prompt / Avoidance Language

Use when the generator is likely to drift:

> Avoid photorealism, gritty fantasy, dark arena mood, modern stadium branding, corporate event signage, sponsor logos, vehicles, urban roads except where explicitly needed as a false-line crossing, military/security imagery, hostile hazards, gore, disaster scenes, cluttered unreadable signage, excessive tiny text, neon colours, flat icon-only vector art, collage layouts, numbered panels, Patch, Bit, Bot, contestants, judges, officials, spectators, stakeholders, or extra characters.

## 19. Asset Acceptance Checklist

Before accepting a new environment asset, check:

- Does it clearly belong to the same sunny blue-and-gold tournament world?
- Is the trial type or location readable at a glance?
- Are starts, finishes, markers, standards, or route logic visible where needed?
- Does the image preserve the warm countryside setting rather than drifting into stadium, office, or generic fantasy?
- Are signs and symbols sparse, useful, and legible?
- Are the forms simplified and reusable enough for source-asset work?
- Are flowers, tents, banners, hills, paths, and brass/gold posts consistent with the existing pack?
- Are there no unwanted characters or extra narrative elements?
- Is the mood cheerful, fair, and orderly rather than threatening or chaotic?
- Does the image match the accepted editorial/vector-adjacent style with light painterly shading?
