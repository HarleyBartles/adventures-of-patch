# Silk Frame Bible

Version: v0.5 extracted frame-transition bible  
State: `minimal_provisional_frame_language`  
Operation class: deterministic no-credit frame bible

## Function

Silk's frame grammar makes adversarial pressure testing visible. Her panels are a physical route, but the route does not only run inside panels. It also runs through panel boundaries. The comic-page architecture itself is part of Silk's test corridor.

## Domain

Silk's frame domain is an ordinary apartment/service corridor weaponised into a red-team test space: bare floorboards, plain plaster walls, cheap apartment doors, exposed conduit, taped wiring, bare hanging lightbulbs, utility cupboards, radiators, simple alarm boxes, maintenance hatches, improvised anchors, and small pressure-test devices.

Do not drift into cave, dungeon, bunker, sci-fi base, warehouse, cyberpunk maze, or horror labyrinth aesthetics.

## Panel grammar

Use thin, edgy, risky panels linked by red string, but do not treat the string as a decorative overlay or a flat route map. The red string is a test rig: it anchors to panel corners, tensions broken borders, braces unsafe seams, marks failed crossings, holds gaps open, and proves which discontinuities can safely carry traversal.

Panels may contain corridor fragments, but the defining Silk grammar is broken-boundary traversal.

Characters must not simply move inside one panel and then appear in another. When a route crosses panels, the crossing point must be visible as an opened, cracked, torn, bent, pulled-apart, propped, hinged, peeled, or tensioned boundary. A body, hand, foot, rope, tool, prop, clamp, hook, or tension line should make the boundary traversal legible.

If a panel narrows, Patch and Silk squeeze through. If it drops, Silk rappels down. If it fractures, the break itself becomes the traversal challenge. If it dead-ends, they stop and reroute. If a switchback gutter is needed, it is a real crawlspace or test-corridor bypass through the page architecture.

The route should feel unsafe before testing and readable only after Silk pressure-tests it. Silk does not merely discover a shortcut; she proves which broken seams, gutters, cracks, and boundary gaps hold under adversarial interpretation. The final route is not shorter; it is safer.

## Transition catalogue

Use these as a grab bag of transition options when designing Silk frame references. A single proof image may demonstrate several transition types without yet defining the final story route.

### Peekhole / behind-frame continuity

Positive steer: one panel shows the entrance to a hidden pipe, crawl route, or low service void; another panel shows the same passage continuing behind the frame barrier or reappearing from behind a different panel. The viewer should understand that the route passes behind the visible comic frame.

Negative steer: do not make it a normal doorway, normal secret tunnel, or unrelated hidden passage. It must feel connected to the panel structure, not just to the apartment architecture.

QA read: can the viewer tell that two separated panels are showing different views of the same behind-frame passage?

### Crumbled crack climb-through

Positive steer: rough plaster, brick, lath, or wall material has broken enough to create a climb-through or squeeze-through point. This is the one transition type where ordinary crumbling wall damage may dominate.

Negative steer: do not let every transition become generic crumbly wall damage. Use this sparingly so it remains one transition style rather than the whole Silk grammar.

QA read: does this read as a deliberate climb/squeeze crack, not just background decay?

### Propped frame aperture

Positive steer: steel rods, timber braces, wedges, jacks, or improvised props hold a thick white comic-frame lip open. The aperture should look temporarily engineered and risky.

Negative steer: do not make it ordinary building scaffolding with no relation to the comic border. The white panel edge must visibly be the thing being held open.

QA read: can the viewer identify the prop as holding open the frame boundary itself?

### Pulled-apart shaft

Positive steer: a central vertical panel-boundary split is stretched into a climb or rappel shaft. Red string, rings, clamps, and anchor points hold the shaft under tension. This transition gives the slide conceptual height.

Negative steer: do not make the shaft a normal stairwell, ladder room, elevator shaft, or generic platform-game vertical corridor.

QA read: does the shaft read as a broken page-boundary feature rather than a room?

### Peeled white-border flap

Positive steer: the thick white comic border peels back like stiff physical material, exposing dark negative space behind the page or a crawlable edge. The peeled lip is the passage.

Negative steer: do not make it look like cracked paint, loose wallpaper, or broken plaster alone. The graphic frame border must be the object peeling.

QA read: does the white border visibly peel away from the panel plane?

### Hinged / bent frame lip

Positive steer: a frame edge bends or hinges under load, held by clamps, hooks, or red string. It should feel stiff, awkward, and temporarily forced open.

Negative steer: do not make it floppy cloth, a curtain, a soft ribbon, or a random broken wall. It is panel material under mechanical strain.

QA read: does the bend make the boundary traversable?

### Tensioned squeeze gutter

Positive steer: red string, rings, clamps, or pulley-like anchors pull two panel edges apart just enough to make a narrow squeeze-route. The seam itself is the path.

Negative steer: do not merely draw red rope across unchanged panels. The rope must visibly deform, hold, or open the gutter.

QA read: is the passage only possible because the gutter is being held open under tension?

### Split-panel careful crossing

Positive steer: one corridor view is split into two offset panel halves. The crack or seam between them is a crossing hazard: uneven, shifted, or unstable. Silk tests it before Patch follows.

Negative steer: do not make it a normal gap between two rooms or a simple broken floor. The image fracture and the physical crossing should be the same thing.

QA read: does the viewer understand that crossing the split is both a page-grammar move and a physical risk?

### Failed seam / blocked fracture

Positive steer: one visible non-route proves Silk rejects unsafe breaks. It may show a collapsed seam, blocked aperture, boarded crack, or red X/check failure marker.

Negative steer: do not overuse failed seams. One strong failure is usually enough. Too many blocked fractures turn the frame into chaos rather than pressure-tested logic.

QA read: does the failure clarify Silk's adversarial testing rather than clutter the composition?

## Central shaft rule

When a central shaft layout is used, the shaft is a panel-boundary traversal feature, not an ordinary room, stairwell, ladder well, elevator shaft, or platform-game shaft.

The shaft must have:

- one clear entry break;
- one clear exit break;
- visible traversal logic between them.

For the current preferred central-shaft composition, the shaft entry is at the lower-left edge of the shaft and the shaft exit is at the upper-right edge of the shaft. Avoid vague extra shaft breaches or floating access points that weaken the route logic.

## Prompt-positive canon block

```text
Use Silk's frame grammar as an adversarial route traversal through a thin, tense, red-string apartment-corridor test route. Panels are physical corridor segments and broken page-boundaries: narrow slivers, peekhole continuity, torn gutters, crumbled crack climb-throughs, cracked seams, squeeze gaps, propped frame apertures, pulled-apart shafts, peeled white-border flaps, hinged or bent frame lips, split-panel crossings, blocked shortcuts, failed seams, tensioned red-string braces, reroutes, and final check pins. Every panel transition visibly crosses or tests the boundary itself rather than merely jumping from one panel to another.
```

## Prompt-negative canon block

```text
Do not make the panels decorative only; the characters must physically traverse or test the panel route and its broken boundaries. Do not let characters simply move inside one panel and appear in another without crossing the visible seam, gutter, crack, split, aperture, flap, hinge, prop, or tensioned break. Do not let every transition become generic crumbly wall damage. Do not use classical left-to-right panels if the test route should divert, fracture, climb, or reroute. Do not turn the corridor into a cave, dungeon, bunker, sci-fi base, warehouse, cyberpunk maze, or horror labyrinth.
```

## QA gates

- Does the panel layout function as the actual route or route-transition test bed?
- Are panel boundaries themselves traversable, not merely decorative dividers?
- Does each panel-to-panel crossing have a visible opened, cracked, torn, bent, pulled-apart, propped, hinged, peeled, or tensioned boundary?
- Do characters visibly cross, test, or prepare the seam, gutter, crack, split, boundary gap, aperture, flap, hinge, prop, or tensioned break rather than simply appearing in adjacent panels?
- If multiple transition types appear, are they visually distinct from one another?
- Are crumbled-wall transitions used sparingly rather than becoming the default?
- Are dead ends and reroutes readable but not chaotic?
- Does Patch physically follow or prepare to follow the tested route?
- Does the environment remain recognisably ordinary and domestic rather than cave, dungeon, bunker, sci-fi base, warehouse, cyberpunk maze, or horror labyrinth?
- If a central shaft is used, does it have one clear entry break and one clear exit break, with no vague extra breaches?
