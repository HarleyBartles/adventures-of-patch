# Adventures of Patch General Style Guide v1.1

Status: user-approved sidecar for the style pattern and anti-pattern sheet pair generated in this session. Intended to live alongside the approved `Approved Style Patterns Sheet v1.1` and `Style Anti-Patterns Sheet v1.1` once landed in the repo/project sources.

## 1. Purpose

This guide defines the general visual style for Adventures of Patch assets across decks, asset sheets, contact sheets, anti-pattern sheets, environments, props, humans, other agents, and supporting visual systems.

The project style should be clear, friendly, purposeful, readable at presentation scale, and coherent across asset types. It should feel like one world rather than a collection of unrelated illustration styles.

Patch is the through-line. Unless an example is specifically about another character class, environment, or prop, Patch is the default exemplar for the project style.

## 3. Core style principles

The Adventures of Patch style is:

- clean, friendly, and purposeful;
- simplified without becoming generic;
- readable at thumbnail and presentation scale;
- built from clear silhouettes and rounded forms;
- finished with thick, smooth, consistent outlines;
- softly shaded with controlled light direction;
- uncluttered and intentional;
- emotionally warm, curious, helpful, and organized;
- consistent across characters, props, environments, and UI-like elements.

The style is not photorealistic, glossy 3D, plush, anime/chibi, gritty, horror, painterly realism, generic clip art, corporate stock illustration, meme style, or over-detailed sci-fi concept art.

## 4. Patch as the style anchor

Patch is the primary visual anchor for the world. Patch should appear as the default style exemplar in general style guidance, quick tests, and project-wide pattern/anti-pattern sheets.

Patch must remain canonical:

- friendly autonomous software-agent protagonist;
- compact rounded proportions;
- teal hoodie with hood antennae;
- off-white face panel;
- simple black oval eyes with small white shine highlights only;
- no white sclera, irises, anime eyes, human eyes, doll eyes, large mascot eyes, or alternate eye styles;
- dark pants;
- teal shoes with white soles;
- teal crossbody bag with visible `>` symbol;
- visible strap crossing the torso whenever the torso is visible;
- clean editorial vector finish with thick rounded outlines and soft shading.

Patch must not be represented as a generic robot, screen-face bot, glossy mascot, metal android, plush toy, photoreal character, or generic AI icon.

In anti-pattern sheets, wrong Patch variants may be shown only as rejected examples. They must be clearly marked as not canon.

## 5. Character class rules

### 5.1 Patch

Patch is singular. There is only one Patch. Do not create Patch clones, Patch-shaped teams, Patch-shaped audiences, or other agents wearing Patchâ€™s hoodie, antennae, bag, `>` symbol, face panel, or full silhouette.

Patch is the protagonist, not a decoration. When Patch appears in a deck scene, he should normally be doing something that carries the lesson: inspecting, asking, checking, receiving, presenting, handing off, validating, learning, being blocked, or applying a rule.

### 5.2 Other agents

Other agents are not Patch. They are clearly robotic bots or agent-shaped workers, but they must remain visually distinct.

Approved other-agent direction:

- clearly robotic or bot-like;
- no hoodie;
- no teal Patch identity palette as the main read;
- no crossbody bag;
- no `>` symbol;
- no Patch face panel or full silhouette;
- no Patch-like antenna hoodie shape;
- role clarity through silhouette, accent color, icon, tool, or body proportions;
- friendly and capable rather than threatening;
- simple screen faces or graphic eyes are acceptable for other agents.

Other agents may appear as specialists, peers, systems, workers, or handoff partners when the story needs them. They should not be used as generic decorative crowd filler.

### 5.3 Humans

Humans should read clearly as humans, not bots and not Patch variants.

Approved human direction:

- simple, friendly editorial cartoon construction;
- readable posture and gesture;
- role-appropriate clothing and props;
- expressive but not extreme faces;
- no photorealistic skin, uncanny facial detail, or cinematic realism;
- no borrowing Patchâ€™s hoodie/bag/antennae silhouette.

Humans should usually represent meaningful counterparties: stakeholders, requesters, reviewers, operators, gatekeepers, approvers, teammates, sponsors, or users.

## 6. Environment style

Environments should support the story, not merely decorate it.

Approved environment direction:

- clear focal point;
- readable layout and perspective;
- logical paths, doors, thresholds, desks, gates, rooms, and surfaces;
- uncluttered composition with controlled visual density;
- purposeful props and signage;
- lighting that guides attention;
- consistent palette and material treatment;
- enough detail to make the place recognizable, but not so much that the scene becomes noisy.

Environment sheets should not include named characters unless the sheet is explicitly about interaction, scale, or story staging. Pure location sheets should focus on the place.

Avoid:

- overly busy rooms;
- every surface covered;
- irrelevant object stuffing;
- generic empty rooms with no story clue;
- embedded text that says nothing;
- signage overload;
- impossible room geometry;
- blocked paths without story reason;
- inconsistent lighting;
- palette drift;
- mixed rendering styles;
- detail overload.

## 7. Prop style

Props should be clear, functional, and story-relevant.

Approved prop direction:

- readable silhouette;
- obvious function or intentionally mysterious function when the story needs it;
- simple forms with believable construction;
- correct scale for Patch, humans, other agents, and the environment;
- physically supported when held, worn, placed, plugged in, opened, closed, or attached;
- purposeful labels, icons, screens, or markings;
- materials that match the Adventures of Patch finish;
- no unnecessary greebles, stickers, buttons, straps, or duplicate parts.

Props are not random set dressing. A prop should either carry the concept, support the action, clarify the role, or reinforce the world.

Avoid:

- floating props;
- hands not actually gripping objects;
- detached straps, lids, wheels, handles, hinges, or cables;
- duplicate accessories;
- unclear function;
- random glowing parts;
- unreadable tiny controls;
- props that change design between images without reason;
- props that are too realistic, too noisy, or from a different world.

## 8. Line, shape, and silhouette

Use:

- thick rounded outlines;
- smooth, confident linework;
- simplified readable shapes;
- clear silhouettes;
- rounded corners and friendly forms;
- modular shape logic for props and bots;
- enough asymmetry for personality without becoming messy.

Avoid:

- sketchy, scratchy, trembling, or jagged outlines;
- inconsistent line weights;
- thin spindly limbs unless specifically canonical for an asset;
- overly sharp aggressive angles;
- tiny unreadable details;
- flat lifeless shapes with no hierarchy;
- over-complicated mechanisms;
- silhouette crowding.

## 9. Shading, texture, and finish

Use:

- soft controlled shading;
- simple cel-like or editorial shading;
- clear light direction;
- subtle material cues;
- clean gradient or shadow support where useful;
- enough depth to avoid flatness, without realism drift.

Avoid:

- heavy photorealistic lighting;
- harsh airbrush shine;
- metallic realism on everything;
- noisy/gritty textures;
- glossy toy rendering;
- painterly concept-art finish;
- broken shadows;
- inconsistent highlights;
- over-rendered surfaces.

## 10. Color and palette

The world should feel cohesive and readable. Patchâ€™s identity colors are protected. Other assets may use role-specific accents, but should not steal Patchâ€™s identity.

Use:

- coherent project palette;
- controlled accents for role, status, or function;
- semantic colors sparingly;
- warm, friendly overall tone;
- clean contrast for readability;
- color harmony across scenes.

Avoid:

- oversaturated neon unless the frame specifically needs it;
- muddy desaturated palettes that drain the friendly tone;
- harsh black-and-white contrast that hurts readability;
- random palette shifts between related assets;
- assigning Patchâ€™s teal identity to other agents as their main read;
- using color as clutter rather than information.

## 11. Composition and readability

Every image should be easy to understand quickly.

Use:

- one clear focal point;
- readable staging;
- intentional eye lines and gestures;
- clear depth and spacing;
- purposeful negative space;
- large enough faces, props, signs, and state markers to survive slide projection;
- simple background support;
- clean hierarchy between Patch, other actors, props, and environment.

Avoid:

- crowded all-over compositions;
- important details hidden behind props or people;
- tangent lines and awkward overlaps;
- accidental cropped subjects;
- duplicate background characters;
- UI or signage blocking key content;
- mismatched scale;
- visual noise that hides the teaching point.

## 12. Text, labels, signs, and UI

Text inside generated images should be short, purposeful, and part of the world.

Approved uses:

- short signs;
- route labels;
- checkpoint labels;
- dashboard state labels;
- receipt stamps;
- mission board headings;
- file labels;
- warning panels;
- compact checklists;
- icons that clarify role or function.

Avoid:

- lorem ipsum;
- motivational filler text;
- paragraphs inside images;
- random serial numbers with no story meaning;
- unreadable screen content;
- real-world brand logos;
- watermark-like text;
- text pasted in as decoration;
- embedded text that does not tell or support the story.

Precise explanatory wording belongs in PPTX text, presenter notes, or sidecar documents, not inside dense generated image panels.

## 13. Physical logic

All assets should obey basic physical plausibility unless an explicit frame-world rule says otherwise.

Check:

- arms, hands, fingers, legs, feet, and joints are logical;
- held objects are actually held;
- worn objects are attached to bodies;
- props rest on surfaces or are visibly supported;
- furniture and doors make sense;
- cables connect to plausible places;
- straps connect to bags;
- shadows, reflections, and light sources match;
- characters face plausible directions;
- objects do not pass through walls, bodies, furniture, or each other;
- scale relationships are believable.

Reject or repair:

- extra arms or fingers;
- merged limbs;
- impossible poses;
- floating mugs, tools, tablets, or bags;
- detached straps or accessories;
- clipping through furniture;
- duplicate props;
- accidental hats, badges, stickers, backpacks, tails, or gadgets;
- random objects smuggled into the scene.

## 14. Tone boundaries

The Adventures of Patch world should feel curious, reliable, organized, and helpful.

Avoid:

- villainous Patch;
- criminal/hacker-in-hoodie framing;
- horror imagery;
- gore or body horror;
- threatening other agents;
- grim military realism unless the frame has been explicitly approved and softened into the project style;
- cynical meme tone;
- sarcasm-heavy visual language;
- real politics or real-world campaign symbols;
- real weapons or violent escalation as default imagery.

Conflict is allowed, but it should usually read as process friction, missing context, unclear requirements, unsafe handoff, blocked route, failed validation, or learning pressure rather than menace.

## 15. Asset-class quick rules

### Patch

Default exemplar. Protected canon. Never generic robot.

### Other agents

Clearly bots. Not Patch-shaped. No hoodie, teal identity, bag, `>` symbol, or Patch silhouette.

### Humans

Clearly human. Friendly, simple, role-specific. Not photoreal and not bot-like.

### Environments

Story-relevant, spatially coherent, uncluttered, readable, and consistent.

### Props

Functional, attached or supported, readable, and useful to the story.

### UI/screens/signage

Short, legible, purposeful, and diegetic. Never filler.

## 18. Approval checklist

Before accepting a generated asset or sheet, check:

- Does Patch remain canonical whenever Patch appears?
- Is Patch the through-line for general style examples?
- Are humans clearly human?
- Are other agents clearly robotic and not Patch-like?
- Is the line weight thick, smooth, and consistent?
- Are shapes simplified but not generic?
- Is shading soft and controlled?
- Is the palette coherent and on-world?
- Are props functional and physically supported?
- Are environments readable and not overstuffed?
- Is text short, purposeful, and legible?
- Are there any accidental extra limbs, props, badges, bags, hats, tails, or background figures?
- Would the image still read well at presentation scale?
- Does every element serve the story, role, or function?

## 19. When in doubt

Simplify. Remove clutter. Preserve Patch. Keep the world friendly. Make the purpose clear.

If a style choice pushes an asset away from Adventures of Patch, do not accept it as final. Regenerate, repair, or mark it as an anti-pattern.
